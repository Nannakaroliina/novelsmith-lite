Variant of `mystery.md` for Nordic noir and Finnish crime: a police procedural relocated into a welfare state, where the case diagnoses a social fault line, the season is an antagonist, the ending may stay open, and (Finnish) humour and criminal-POV strands sit inside the gloom. Load **after** `mystery.md`; fair-play rules, clue economy, suspect matrix and the fairness audit are not repeated here. Where this file and `mystery.md` disagree, this file wins; where either disagrees with `## ⚓ Anchored` in `book/bible/premise.md`, the author wins.

## 1. Lineage in 6 lines

1. Sjöwall & Wahlöö's Martin Beck series (1965–75) founded the form: ten police procedurals written as a critique of the Swedish welfare state, "a scalpel cutting open the belly" of a bourgeois model of it.
2. The move that defines the genre: take the Anglo-American procedural and relocate it into a welfare society, then challenge that order **from within** rather than reassure the reader that it holds.
3. The 1990s wave (Mankell's Wallander, Indriðason's Erlendur, Nesser's Van Veeteren) carried the form on the police procedural: squad, bureaucracy, cases that ripple across books.
4. The investigator became an everyman whose wounds are bodily and psychological (Beck's stomach, Wallander's diabetes, Hole's alcoholism); the body indexes the society.
5. *Femikrimi* (Marklund, Läckberg, Blædel, Holt, Lehtolainen) reversed the passive female portrayal and made violence against women a central theme.
6. Use Wallander and Hole only as **pattern references** (morose everyman, decaying body, lost-paradise nostalgia); never borrow their plots, cases or set pieces.

## 2. Nordic noir contract

The base contract in `mystery.md` → *Contract* still applies (crime, closed suspect set, fair play, exposure). This variant adds the following, all checkable at stage 3:

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Crime as social function | The crime MUST be explicable as a product of a social condition, not only a personal motive. The personal motive still exists (fair play), but the case exposes what made it possible. |
| Named fault line | `premise.md` MUST name one welfare-state fault line the case cuts open: violence against women, child-welfare failure, xenophobia, corruption, care-system collapse, or one the author specifies. The fault line is the controlling idea's *evidence*, shown by the case, never narrated. |
| Investigator's body | The investigator carries a bodily index of social dysfunction (stomach, diabetes, alcohol, sleeplessness, a job-made injury). It recurs; it does not get cured. |
| Landscape and season | Cold, desolate, lightless settings are expected by readers. Landscape "a character in itself" that presses on the town and mirrors inner states. Season MUST act on the plot (see §4 season-turn scene). |
| Tone | Brooding, pessimistic, "rarely triumphant." *Folkhem* nostalgia (the lost paradise of social responsibility, collectivism, egalitarianism) is shown as unreachable, not restored. |
| Prose | Spare, direct, unsentimental, plain; the kind that survives translation. |
| Endings | Ambiguous or open endings are permitted. Exposure of the culprit is still owed; justice is not. |
| Moral grey | Both sides. Perpetrators have social causes; institutions and investigators have compromises. No infallible police, no good-vs-evil simplification ("copaganda" is a listed reader complaint). |
| Institutional limits | The investigator is bounded by bureaucracy, budgets, hierarchy, prosecutors, politics. At least one of these obstructs the case materially (see §4). |

**Non-negotiables when secondary** (Nordic noir as the colouring genre on another base): social fault line in the premise; season acting on plot; plain prose; no uniformly positive valence; ending may be open.

## 3. The Finnish inflection (priority variant)

Finnish crime fiction is the plugin's priority variant. It inherits §2 and shifts it as follows. Treat these as **expected**, not optional, when `premise.md` says "Finnish crime."

### 3.1 Tonal shifts
- **Humour inside the gloom.** Finnish crime fiction "has always" used humour; the Swedish model reads "so depressing and sinister" by contrast. Finnish noir has "many different shades on top of just black." Humour MUST appear, and it comes as understatement, deadpan and situational absurdity, never as quips (see §5).
- **More hard-boiled.** Violence is on the page and consequential; the investigator is closer to Chandler's mean streets than to the Golden Age study.
- **Good does not always win.** The reader is owed exposure; the culprit may walk, the institution may bury it, the investigator may lose more than the case.
- **"A certain Finnish craziness."** One strand of the book may run on a logic that is grotesque, absurd or wildly disproportionate (a feud over nothing, an elaborate scheme for a small gain, a body disposed of with comic incompetence). It is played straight.
- **Themes in the notes:** alcohol, violence, social malaise, wartime history. Alcohol in particular is both texture and causation.

### 3.2 Structural shifts
- **Criminal-POV strands are common.** A second POV from the criminal's (or victim's) world is normal here, not a cheat. Constraint from `mystery.md` → *Fairness audit* still holds: the culprit's thoughts may be shown only if the author has decided the book is a howcatchem/whydunit rather than a whodunit, or if the criminal-POV character is a *different* criminal than the one the whodunit exposes. Record the choice in `premise.md`.
- **Laconic dialogue is load-bearing.** Dialogue is "realistic, simple and particularly important" precisely because Finns are not talkative. Every line costs; silence is a reply. Budget in §5.
- **Winter is an antagonist and a source of conflict.** The seasonal clock is a plot engine: polar night compresses hours of light and tempers; the thaw reveals bodies kept under ice and snow; midsummer empties towns and offices; the freeze closes roads and water. At least one plot turn MUST be caused by season, not merely set in it.
- **Setting.** Small-town and rural Finland (the north, Lapland, lake and forest country) alongside Helsinki. A rural setting tightens the suspect set (everyone knows everyone), slows institutional response, and makes the investigator's private life public.
- **Investigators with their own code.** Lehtolainen's heroine "does not always follow the law, but knows her own moral values." The investigator's private code and the law MUST diverge at least once in a way that costs something.

### 3.3 Roster to pattern on (patterns only, no plots)
- Matti Joensuu's Harjunpää: the weary Helsinki policeman inside a strained institution.
- Leena Lehtolainen's Maria Kallio: female investigator, private moral code over the letter of the law, private life as a parallel thread.
- Harri Nykänen (the source of the "shades on top of black" observation) for the humour-in-gloom register.
- Any further names the author supplies in `premise.md` or `style.md`.

### 3.4 Finnish texture to reach for
Use concrete, named things rather than "the cold" and "the forest." Items marked **(verify with the author)** are not in the research notes; they are prompts for the author to confirm or correct, never facts to assert in prose until confirmed.

| Texture | Status |
|---|---|
| Long lightless winters; bodies under ice and snow revealed by the spring thaw | in notes |
| Alcohol as texture and cause; social malaise; wartime history as buried backstory | in notes |
| Small towns, the north, Lapland, lake and forest country; Helsinki | in notes |
| Kaamos (polar night) and the midnight sun as terms for the light; exact dates and latitudes per location | verify with the author |
| Sauna as the place where things get said or not said | verify with the author |
| Reindeer husbandry, herding cooperatives, round-ups, earmarks, compensation disputes | verify with the author |
| Snowmobiles, ice roads, lake ice thickness, frozen bogs as routes and hiding places | verify with the author |
| The eastern border with Russia: smuggling, border guard, cross-border family ties | verify with the author |
| Finnish police / prosecutor structure (who leads a pre-trial investigation, who charges, forensic routing, which unit handles what) | verify with the author; the notes give no structure, and procedural errors cost reader trust |
| Everyman's rights (free movement and foraging on private land) as a plot affordance | verify with the author |
| Summer cottages (mökki) as the empty, unwatched place | verify with the author |
| Welfare bureaucracy: child protection, social office, unemployment, care homes, waiting lists | verify with the author for current names and procedures |

## 4. Obligatory scenes and structure deltas vs the base mystery

All of `mystery.md` → *Obligatory scenes* remain (crime on the page early, suspects laid out, false solution near the midpoint, low point, reveal that explains means and motive, short denouement). This variant **adds** four scenes and **relaxes** three outcomes.

### Added (MUST exist in `book/outline/scenes.yaml`, tag `subplot: social` / `criminal` / `institution` / `season`)
1. **Social-theme reveal that parallels the whodunit solution.** At or near the reveal (~85–90 %), a second disclosure lands: what the institution, town or system did or failed to do that made the crime possible. It is discovered through the case (a file, a missed report, a budget line, a neighbour's silence), never announced by the narrator. The whodunit answers "who"; this answers "how could this happen here."
2. **At least one scene from the criminal's or victim's world.** A scene whose POV or close focus belongs to the perpetrator's or victim's milieu, showing the social condition from the inside. Obeys the fairness constraint in §3.2.
3. **Institutional-obstacle scene.** A superior, prosecutor, budget, jurisdiction, politics, or a sister agency blocks or bends the investigation. The obstacle is not a straw man; the person enforcing it has a defensible reason.
4. **Season-turn scene.** The season changes state (first snow, polar night settling, ice going out, thaw, midsummer) and the change itself causes a plot turn: a body appears, a road closes, a witness leaves, a scene becomes reachable or unreachable.

### Relaxed (permitted outcomes; record the author's choice in `premise.md` → ending)
- **Justice may not be delivered.** Exposure is owed; conviction, arrest or punishment are not. The culprit may be known and untouchable.
- **The detective may expose without resolving.** The case closes; the condition that produced it does not.
- **The denouement may be bleak.** No restoration of order, no reconciliation scene, no epilogue that softens. The investigator's private thread may end lower than it began.

### Beat-table adjustments (`book/outline/acts.md`)
- The investigator's private decline/strain thread MUST have beats at ~25 / ~50 / ~75 % like a character arc, running parallel to and usually *worsening* with the case.
- The season-turn scene should sit at a structural beat (midpoint or ~75 %) so weather does the work a coincidence would otherwise do.
- Subplots are expected (private life, institution, criminal strand). A Nordic/Finnish book with no subplot fails the contract.

## 5. Drafting rules (stage 4; reviewers quote violations at stage 5)

**Dialogue**
- Laconic budget: prefer lines of **≤ 12 words**. A line over 25 words needs a reason the reviewer can name. **NEVER** speeches; no character explains the theme, the society or their feelings in a paragraph of talk.
- Silence and action are valid replies. Prefer a character not answering, changing the subject, or doing something with their hands over a reply.
- Questions can go unanswered. Interrogations stall. People give up information grudgingly, late, or by accident.
- Tags: "said" or none. No adverbs on tags.
- Before/after:
  > Before: "I'm not going to pretend this doesn't frighten me, Kari. But we both know that what happened to her is bigger than one man's cruelty. It's this whole town."
  > After: "You scared?" / He put the kettle on. "Her brother called again." / "And?" / "I didn't pick up."

**Prose**
- Plain declaratives. Short paragraphs. Nouns over adjectives. Prefer repeating the clear word to cycling synonyms.
- **Weather and light in every scene**, in specific terms (minus degrees, hours of light, the colour of the snow at 2 p.m., the sound of ice, slush, the smell of a wet dog in a car). Not "it was cold and dark." If a scene has no weather or light note, it is not finished.
- Mirror inner state through setting at most **once per scene**; after that the landscape just is what it is (StoryScope: AI mirrors feeling through setting more often than humans).
- Emotion: name it plainly or show it in action; **NEVER** the body-cue-then-label pair (`mystery.md` and `de-ai` rules apply).

**Humour**
- Understatement, deadpan, the absurd played straight. **NEVER** quips, banter rhythms, or a character who exists to be funny.
- Test: the joke would survive being read aloud with no change of expression.
- Before/after:
  > Before: "Well, that's one way to ruin a sauna night," Jussi quipped, grinning.
  > After: The body had been in the sauna about a week. Jussi turned off the stove. "He'd have wanted it warm."

**Valence and resolution**
- **NEVER** a therapeutic reconciliation scene (a talk that heals a rift, a family that reconnects because of the case). Rifts stay, or shift sideways.
- Valence is not uniformly positive: at least a third of scene outcomes in `scenes.yaml` are `no-and`, and the investigator's private thread trends down or sideways.
- The investigator's private life is a parallel decline/strain thread (health, drink, marriage, a child, money, sleep). It gets scenes; it does not get solved by the case ending.
- Arguments do not resolve inside the scene they start in.

**Finnish specifics**
- Keep Finnish names, places and terms as the author gives them in `style.md`; do not translate or gloss them in-line unless the author's sample passages do.
- Institutional detail (ranks, who charges, forensic routing) only from `book/bible/world/*.md` after the author has confirmed it (§3.4).

## 6. Why this variant counters AI defaults

The measured tendencies of AI-written fiction (StoryScope; Tian et al.) are, almost item for item, the things this genre forbids. Writing to the contract is the de-AI pass.

| Nordic / Finnish convention | AI default it pushes against |
|---|---|
| Open or ambiguous ending; exposure without justice; bleak denouement | Tidy resolution, protagonist-driven endings (69 % vs 46 % human), endings of internal acceptance (47 % vs 27 %), Claude's preference for epilogues |
| Laconic dialogue budget; silence as reply; no speeches | Dialogue used for philosophical debate (59 % vs 34 %), therapy-speak, mic-drop closers, characters explaining their feelings |
| Humour-in-gloom, criminal-POV strand, investigator with own code vs the institution | Uniform voice (Claude: most uniform voice of the models measured), morally simple protagonists (ambivalent 38 % vs 59 %), "reverent, continuist" treatment of convention |
| Season as antagonist with a season-turn scene at a structural beat; private decline thread | "Notably flat event escalation" (Claude), climax and setback arriving early, lower arousal after the midpoint |
| Social fault line shown by the case; social-theme reveal discovered, never narrated | Narrator states the theme outright (77 % vs 52 %) |
| Plain declaratives, plain emotion naming, one setting-mirror per scene | Emotion via bodily sensation (81 % vs 38 %), setting that mirrors feeling, smell imagery, wisdom-summary closers |
| Required subplots (private, institutional, criminal) | No subplots (79 % vs 57 % human) |
| Not uniformly positive valence; arguments unresolved in-scene | Homogeneously positive valence; conflicts that dissolve |

Practical consequence: a Nordic/Finnish draft that reads "AI-written" has almost always broken the genre contract first. Fix the contract breach and the surface tells mostly go with it.

## 7. Reviewer checklist (genre specialist; run at stages 3, 5 and 7)

Quote evidence; propose diffs; severity P0 = contract breach, P1 = convention missing, P2 = texture. Never contradict `## ⚓ Anchored`.

- [ ] (3) `premise.md` names one welfare-state fault line, and the outline shows it *through the case* (a scene or clue carries it); P0 if the fault line exists only in the premise text.
- [ ] (3) The four added scenes exist in `scenes.yaml` (social reveal, criminal/victim world, institutional obstacle, season turn) with ids; P1 for each missing.
- [ ] (3) The season-turn scene *causes* a plot turn (not just atmosphere); P1.
- [ ] (3) The investigator's private strain thread has beats at ~25/50/75 % and does not resolve with the case; P1.
- [ ] (3) The ending type is recorded (justice delivered / exposure only / culprit untouchable / bleak) and is not a tidy restoration unless anchored; P1.
- [ ] (3) If a criminal POV exists, the fairness constraint in §3.2 is satisfied and the choice is recorded in `premise.md`; P0 if the whodunit culprit's thoughts are on the page.
- [ ] (3, 5) At least one subplot beyond the case; P1 if none.
- [ ] (5) Dialogue budget: sample three scenes; lines over 25 words are listed with a reason or flagged; any speech explaining theme or feelings is P1.
- [ ] (5) Every scene carries a specific weather/light note; list scenes without one; P2 each.
- [ ] (5) Humour present (Finnish variant) and in the understatement register; quips and banter flagged; P2.
- [ ] (5) No therapeutic reconciliation scene; no argument resolved in the scene it starts; P1.
- [ ] (5) Setting-mirrors-feeling appears at most once per scene; P2 over that.
- [ ] (5, 7) Institutional and procedural detail traces to a confirmed row in `book/bible/world/*.md`; anything from §3.4 marked "verify with the author" and used unconfirmed is P1.
- [ ] (7) Valence across `scenes.yaml`: at least a third `no-and`; private thread trends down or sideways; P1 if the book is uniformly positive after the midpoint.
- [ ] (7) The social-theme reveal lands near the whodunit reveal and is discovered, not narrated; no character or narrator states the theme in a sentence; P0 if the theme is stated.
