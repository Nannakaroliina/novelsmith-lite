Mystery / crime contract: a fair-play puzzle with a closed suspect set, every clue on the page, a false solution at the midpoint, and the criminal exposed by detection, not luck.

Nordic noir and Finnish crime are a tonal overlay on this file, not a replacement: load `references/mystery-nordic-finnish.md` only when `premise.md` names that variant.
Apply this file first; the variant file says which rules below it relaxes (ambiguous endings, social diagnosis, criminal-POV strands).

## 1. Non-negotiables when secondary

When mystery is the added element (e.g. "romantic suspense", "fantasy mystery", a literary novel with a murder), the subplot MUST still deliver:

- A crime with a victim, and a closed set of at least 3 suspects the reader has met before the halfway point.
- A solution reachable from clues on the page. NEVER solve it by confession-from-nowhere, coincidence, or a culprit introduced after ~50%.
- The investigating character actually detects: at least one deduction the reader could have made first.
- Exposure of the criminal before the end. The base genre decides whether justice follows; exposure is not optional.
- Every red herring the subplot raised gets explained.

## 2. Contract

| Element | Mystery promise |
|---|---|
| Core value | Justice ↔ injustice (Story Grid Crime). Controlling idea: justice prevails when the investigator outwits the perpetrator and reveals the truth; its inverse is tyranny: the perpetrator outwits the investigator. |
| Core need / emotion | Safety / **intrigue**, then the "of course" satisfaction of the solved puzzle. |
| Ending promise | The criminal is exposed (core event). Readers feel cheated if not. Whether they are *punished* varies by subgenre (§6). |
| The reader's equal chance | The reader holds every fact the detective holds at the moment of solution. A reread must show the clues were visible: "I should have seen it." |
| Surprise + inevitability | The reveal must be both unpredicted and, in hindsight, the only answer. A predictable ending is a failure; an *unfair* one is unforgivable. |
| Conventions | A MacGuffin (what the criminal wants), investigative red herrings, "making it personal" for the investigator, a clock. |

Write these into `book/bible/premise.md` under promise/ending. If the author anchors a departure (e.g. the criminal escapes), note the trade-off once and respect it.

## 3. Fair-play rules (modern checklist)

Condensed from Knox's Decalogue, Van Dine's Twenty Rules and the Detection Club oath. Stage 3 (outline) and stage 5 (critique).

- [ ] **Culprit prominent and early.** A substantial character the reader meets in the first third, present through the book and at the climax. NEVER someone mentioned once and forgotten.
- [ ] **No late suspects.** The suspect set is closed by ~50%. Suspects may be *eliminated* late (including by a second death), never added.
- [ ] **Culprit is not a POV-thought character.** The reader may not have ridden inside the murderer's head during the murder-relevant moments. (The narrator-detective may NEVER hide a thought that matters to the solution; see §7.)
- [ ] **The detective really detects.** Solution by observation and reasoning. NEVER by coincidence, accident, lucky overheard conversation, a confession the detective did not force, "feminine intuition", séance, mind-reading, or any "mumbo-jumbo".
- [ ] **A coincidence may start the story; it may NEVER solve it.**
- [ ] **Every clue the detective uses has been shown to the reader** at the moment the detective found it, in the detective's own words or on the page.
- [ ] **No unknown poisons, gadgets or secret passages** unless planted and explained before use; at most one secret room; no twins/doubles unless the reader was prepared.
- [ ] **Means and method scientifically plausible**; no secret societies doing the murdering; one culprit (a minor accomplice is allowed); murder (or an equally grave crime) is the default.
- [ ] **Solution hidden in plain sight**, not in material the reader never saw.
- [ ] **No hackneyed devices** unless deliberately subverted: cigarette-butt comparison, forged fingerprints, dummy-figure alibi, the dog that did not bark, twin substitution, knockout drops, locked room unlocked after the police break in, word-association test, cipher. The dying message invites "why not just write the name?"; use it only with a reason the victim could not.

How subgenres hold these:

| Subgenre | Position |
|---|---|
| Cozy, traditional | Keep every rule. The puzzle is the product. |
| Hardboiled / PI | Chandler's position: give murder back to people who commit it for reasons; realism over props. The quest outranks the solution, so the solution may be modest, but it still may NOT be luck, and the detective still may NOT withhold. |
| Noir | Rules relaxed further: the protagonist may be complicit, endings untidy. Exposure still happens; the reader still gets the facts. |
| Ellery Queen devices | **Challenge to the Reader**: the narrative pauses at ~80–85% and states that the reader now has every clue. Use it only if the clue ledger (§5) is airtight. **Double solution**: the first solution is correct as far as it goes; a second complication resolves the case further *without contradicting* the first. Distinct from the *false* solution of §4, which is wrong. |

## 4. Obligatory scenes and placement

Story Grid Crime obligatory moments mapped onto the `book/outline/acts.md` beat table. Enter these in the "mystery overlay" row.

| % | Beat | What MUST happen |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | Opening | Investigator in their world; wound/flaw visible; the victim preferably seen alive among the future suspects. |
| ≤20 (≈page 50) | **Inciting crime** | A body (or grave crime) on the page. Cozy: chapter 1–3; never later than page 50 in any subgenre. Hold it only if the reader has already attached to the sleuth. |
| 10–25 | Investigator commits | A reason to pursue that is personal and plausible (job, stake, doubt that police will solve it). Suspects laid out; each has a first scene. |
| 20–50 | **Progressive complications** | Clues found and shown; suspects lie; red herrings bloom; "making it personal" (the investigator becomes a target, or someone they love is implicated). Clock stated. |
| ~25–40 | **Speech in praise of the villain** | Someone (not the culprit) shows the reader the criminal's cleverness or the crime's cunning. Can be a scene demonstrating it rather than a speech. |
| 37–50 | **Discovery of the MacGuffin** | The investigator learns what the criminal wants, or thinks they do. |
| ~50 | **First / false solution** | The investigator names a culprit on a complete-seeming chain of reasoning. Confident, reasonable, wrong. |
| 50–75 | Collapse | New fact (often a second death or a broken alibi) destroys the first solution; suspects shuffle. |
| ~75 | **Low point** | Investigator wrong, discredited, or personally hit. The "wound" is pressed. Story Grid/Save the Cat All Is Lost. |
| 75–85 | Reframe | One clue already on the page is seen rightly. Optional Challenge to the Reader. |
| 85–95 | **Exposure of the criminal** (core event) | The investigator explains means, motive, opportunity, how every clue fits, and why each cleared suspect is cleared. May be a gathering, a confrontation or a trap. |
| 90–98 | **Justice or escape** | Arrest, death, flight, or a deliberate escape (noir). Subgenre decides; the author's anchored ending overrides. |
| 98–100 | **Denouement** | ≤ 1 scene. Return to the investigator's life, the hobby, the community, the wound. NEVER a 20-page wind-down. |

Placement rules:
- Body by ~20% / page 50. If `scenes.yaml` shows no `beat: inciting_crime` by then, flag P1 at stage 3.
- First solution present and wrong by ~50%. If the outline has no false solution, flag P1.
- Denouement ≤ 1 scene after the justice-or-escape beat. Count scenes after `beat: exposure`; more than two is P2.
- Suspects' first appearances all before 50%. The culprit's first appearance before 33%.
- Scene length: mystery scenes run long (8–15 pages); do not chop them thriller-style every 3–5 pages unless the subgenre is suspense.

## 5. Clue engineering

### Clue types (use at least four kinds across the book)
physical · biological (forensic) · psychological (behaviour, reaction) · timing (alibis, timelines) · background (money, relationships, records) · omission (what is missing or out of place) · the **negative clue** (something that should have happened and did not; use once, not as the keystone).

Distinguish **red herring** (author-planted misdirection) from **false clue** (fabricated by a character). Both need an in-story reason and MUST be explained before the end.

### Plant each important clue three times
1. **Subtle**: buried, casual, attached to something else.
2. **Clearer**: recurs in a new context; a character may misread it aloud.
3. **Obvious in hindsight**: the investigator picks it up for the solution; the reader thinks "of course".

Spread the three plants across acts. NEVER bunch clues in the last quarter. NEVER have the detective find a clue (diary, key, box) and not follow it up in the next scene.

### Hide-in-plain-sight techniques
- Bury the true clue 4th or 5th in a list of five or six items (readers retain the first three and the last).
- Attach it to an emotional moment, so the reader is watching the feeling.
- Mislabel it: a character confidently calls it something else.
- Split it across scenes: half here, half three chapters later; neither half means anything alone.
- Follow it immediately with action or end the chapter on a different hook.
- Let a suspect's reaction carry the clue (an inference clue): keep the reaction specific enough to be read two ways.
- Christie staging: the red herring draws attention *while the clue stays visible in the same frame*.

Before / after, a list burial:

> Before: On the desk lay a train ticket to Oulu, dated Thursday. There was also some post and a mug.
>
> After: The desk held the usual: a mug with a skin on the coffee, two unopened bills, a seed catalogue, a train ticket to Oulu, a dead phone and a child's drawing of a dog. Hanna picked up the drawing.

### Red herrings
- Pair each crucial clue with **two** convincing red herrings.
- Each red herring MUST have an innocent explanation that is delivered on the page before the end (`clues.md` "Explained in scene" column may not be blank at stage 7).
- Red herrings come from real behaviour: a suspect's non-murder secret is the best source.

### Suspects
- 3–7 suspects; five is typical. Readers reliably track four; if you use six or seven, give each a distinguishing tag and eliminate one mid-book.
- Each suspect MUST have: means, motive, opportunity, a personal goal unrelated to the crime, a distinct voice, and a **non-murder secret** they are protecting.
- **Every suspect lies. Exactly one lies about the murder.** The others lie to cover their secrets, which generates the red herrings honestly.
- No suspect may be obviously guilty, and at least one must be a genuine surprise while still satisfying §3.
- Fill the mystery fields in each `book/bible/characters/<slug>.md` (means/motive/opportunity/lie/secret) *before* outlining the investigation.

### The two-timelines rule
- **Murderer's timeline** (what really happened, minute by minute, including what the killer did to cover it): written into `book/bible/timeline.md` under the hidden crime timeline. The reader never sees this document; the drafting agent MUST consult it for every scene touching the crime.
- **Detective's timeline** (what the investigator learns, in what order) *is* `book/outline/scenes.yaml`. The gap between the two timelines is the plot.
- Every item in the murderer's timeline that the solution relies on MUST correspond to at least one clue row in `clues.md`.

### Filling `book/bible/clues.md` and the `scenes.yaml` fields
Clue ledger row: `Clue id` (C01…), `What it is`, `Points to` (culprit / method / motive / timeline), `Planted in` (up to 3 scene ids, in order subtle → clearer → obvious), `Hidden how` (one of the techniques above), `Paid off in` (the exposure scene id), `Status` (planned / planted-1 / planted-2 / planted-3 / paid).
Red herring row: `Id` (R01…), `What it suggests`, `Why it's wrong`, `Explained in scene`.
Suspect matrix row: one per suspect including the culprit; `Lie told` names the lie and the scene; `Cleared in` is the scene where the reader learns the innocent explanation (the culprit's row says "—").

In `scenes.yaml`: `clues_planted: [C03]` on each scene where a plant lands; `clues_paid: [C03, C07]` on the exposure scene; `red_herrings: [R02]` where one is raised *or* explained (note which in the scene `outcome`); `suspect_lies: [<slug>]` on the scene where the lie is spoken. A clue listed in `Paid off in` but never in any `clues_planted` is a stage-5 P0.

## 6. Subgenre contracts

| Subgenre | Sleuth and setting | Content rules | Ending | Words |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cozy** | Amateur, usually a woman, flexible job (self-employed, shop, café); no cops/lawyers/journalists as lead; small town or close community; hobby/craft/food/pet hook that the reader learns about; a friendly law-enforcement contact; a sidekick | Murder off-page, crime scene by impression and euphemism; no gore, explicit sex or much profanity ("could be read by children"); humour; no serial-killer hallmarks (ritual, trophies, sexual frenzy); acceptable methods: poison, blunt object, fall, drowning, stabbing, shooting | Tidy: killer exposed and removed; community restored; no cliffhanger | 60–75k |
| **Traditional / fair-play** | Professional or gifted amateur; closed circle (house, village, train, island) | Full §3 rules; puzzle foremost; violence may be on the page but is not the point | Exposure + justice; explanation scene expected | 70–90k |
| **Hardboiled / PI** | Lone private investigator; urban, corrupt, realistic; spare prose reporting action and dialogue | On-page violence; cynicism; the detective keeps an undeclared, unbuyable honour | Quest over solution; untidy endings allowed; the detective repairs a corner of a corrupt world rather than fixing it | 70–90k |
| **Noir** | "Regular guy" protagonist, not a professional; blurred moral lines; corrupt institutions | Protagonist is doomed or complicit; readers must NOT expect a tidy ending | Unhappy or ambiguous; exposure still happens | 70–90k |
| **Police procedural** | Ensemble squad, not a lone genius; interviews, evidence, forensics, warrants, chain of custody; bureaucracy and institutional limits are plot material; personal arcs woven with the case; cases ripple across books | Legal and procedural accuracy (which agency, rank, who does the autopsy, what needs a warrant) or readers drop you; avoid jargon overload; avoid "copaganda" (infallible police) and good-vs-evil simplification | Solved, but with institutional cost; dominant form since the mid-1970s | 80–100k |
| **Domestic / psychological suspense** | Household stakes (marriage, family, caregivers); often a female protagonist without investigative training whose instincts are dismissed, and the dismissal drives the plot; frequently a whydunit | Unreliable narration allowed only via omission, self-deception, withholding, or impairment (drink, drugs, injury). The POV may lie in dialogue but inner thought MUST match what the character knows. NEVER fake POV knowledge. Plant contradictions between actions and stated beliefs; truth visible on reread | Mid-plot twist popular but not mandatory; ending may be dark; the twist must illuminate, not just shock | 80–100k |

Debut ceiling ~100k words for all rows. Longer literary mysteries 90k+ exist; justify length in `premise.md`.

## 7. Drafting rules (stage 4)

### Interrogation and interview scenes
- The suspect has a goal in the scene other than "answer questions": protect the secret, deflect, get something from the investigator. Write their goal first.
- Nobody answers fully. Realistic witnesses are not chatty with an amateur who has no authority; give the sleuth leverage (a favour, a shared past, a threat, a trade) or let the witness refuse.
- Every suspect scene plants one of: a clue, a lie, a red herring, or a secret's edge. A suspect scene that plants nothing is cut or merged.
- The suspect's lie must be checkable later by something physical or by another witness, not only by the detective "sensing" it.
- Prefer "said" / "asked". Let subtext carry evasions; do not narrate "she was clearly hiding something".

### Writing the detective's mind without cheating
- The reader sees what the detective sees *when* they see it. Describe the found object in the detective's observation, not in a summary after the fact.
- The detective may notice without concluding. Writing "something about the ticket bothered her, but she could not say what" is fair once per clue; naming the conclusion and then hiding it is NEVER fair.
- A first-person or close-third detective MUST NOT think "I knew then who had done it" and then withhold the name for chapters. Either state the reasoning or stay at the level of unease.
- The Watson (sidekick narrator) may be less perceptive than the reader but may not conceal what they thought.
- The detective MUST be wrong at least once, on the page, with reasoning the reader found plausible. The false solution of §4 is the minimum; smaller errors in the first half are good.

Before / after, fair withholding:

> Before: Mikko looked at the boot prints and understood everything. He said nothing. It would keep until he was sure.
>
> After: Mikko looked at the boot prints a second time. Two sets going in, one coming out, both with the same worn left heel. He photographed them and did not yet know what he was looking at.

### Information management
- Keep a per-scene ledger in your head: what the reader knows / what the detective knows / what the culprit knows. Check `book/summaries/running.md` and the character knowledge ledgers before any scene where a character acts on knowledge.
- No character may act on information they have not been shown receiving.
- The culprit's behaviour in every scene MUST be consistent with the hidden timeline in `book/bible/timeline.md`, including what they would *not* know.
- Reveal pacing: a new fact or reversal every scene; a *reframing* of an old fact at least every act. Do not deliver two major reveals in the same scene before the exposure beat.
- Follow up found clues immediately. A found diary read "later" is a reader peeve, not suspense.

### The exposure scene
- It explains: method, motive, opportunity, the order of events, how each clue fits, and why each cleared suspect is cleared. It cannot be dull: stage it with risk (the culprit is present, a trap is set, the detective can still be wrong).
- Confessions, if any, come *after* the evidence is laid out, under pressure, and are not the proof. A villain who boasts while help is on the way is a cliché; cut it.
- Keep the reveal to one scene (two in a procedural). Then denouement ≤ 1 scene.

### Untidiness that is still fair
AI-tells in mystery are tidy plots: suspects' lies that balance neatly, a reveal that resolves every emotional thread, a sleuth who was never embarrassed. Leave an open human thread (a friendship not repaired, a secret the sleuth chooses not to expose) while closing every *plot* thread.

## 8. Reader complaints → the check that prevents them

| Complaint | Prevented by |
|---|---|
| Culprit "picked out of a hat" on one late clue | §3 prominent-early rule; `clues.md` shows ≥3 clue rows pointing at the culprit, each planted 3× across ≥2 acts |
| Unexplained red herrings / dismissed suspects never explained away | `clues.md` "Explained in scene" and "Cleared in" columns filled for every row |
| Detective withholds from the reader | §7 withholding rule; critique quotes any "knew but said nothing" line as P0 |
| Too-easy confession on weak evidence | Exposure scene lays out evidence first; confession never the proof |
| Loose ends "to mirror real life" | Open *human* threads allowed; every plot thread, lie and secret closed; stage-7 thread list in `acts.md` |
| Overlong reveal / 20-page denouement | ≤ 1 reveal scene (+1 in procedural), ≤ 1 denouement scene |
| Coincidence or lucky overhearing does the work | §3; every deduction traceable to a clue row |
| Chatty suspects, sleuth breaking in with no reason | §7 interview rules; sleuth's personal stake stated by ~25% |
| Sleuth surprised by the reveal as if by luck | The reframe beat (75–85%) shows the detective seeing an old clue rightly |
| Unworthy villain: weak motive, absent from the climax | Culprit's `characters/<slug>.md` has Want/Need and a motive that is personal; culprit present in the exposure scene |
| Wrong police procedure, firearms, autopsy | Procedural rows in §6; verify agency, rank and law for the setting (Finnish specifics in the variant file) |
| Cozy with charm but no clever plot | False solution exists; ≥4 clue types; ≥2 red herrings per crucial clue |
| Preachy hobby lectures, pets everywhere | Hobby detail serves a clue or a scene goal; cap lifestyle digressions |
| Cliffhanger to sell the next book | Case closed in this book; series threads stay in the personal arc only |

## 9. Fairness audit (stage 5 critique, repeated at stage 7)

Run with `book/bible/clues.md`, `book/bible/timeline.md`, `book/outline/scenes.yaml` and the manuscript. Quote evidence for every failure; severity in brackets.

- [ ] Every clue in `Paid off in` appears in `clues_planted` of a scene that precedes the exposure scene, and the manuscript text of that scene actually contains it. [P0]
- [ ] Every clue used in the exposure speech has a `clues.md` row; no reasoning step rests on a fact the reader never saw. [P0]
- [ ] Culprit first appears before 33% and is in the exposure scene; culprit is never a POV character during crime-relevant moments. [P0]
- [ ] No suspect first appears after 50%. [P1]
- [ ] Suspect count 3–7; each matrix row complete (means, motive, opportunity, lie, secret). [P1]
- [ ] Exactly one suspect lies about the murder; every other suspect's lie is tied to a non-murder secret that is revealed. [P1]
- [ ] Every red herring row has `Explained in scene` filled and the explanation is in the text. [P1]
- [ ] Each crucial clue has ≥2 plants in the text (3 preferred) across ≥2 acts; none appears only in the final quarter. [P1]
- [ ] A false solution exists near the midpoint, is stated on the page, and collapses by ~75%. [P1]
- [ ] Body or grave crime on the page by 20% (cozy: by chapter 3). [P1]
- [ ] No line where the detective concludes and withholds; no solution step relying on coincidence, overhearing, intuition or unforced confession. [P0]
- [ ] Culprit's on-page behaviour matches the hidden timeline in every scene (spot-check each scene where the culprit appears). [P1]
- [ ] Exposure explains means, motive, opportunity, every clue and every cleared suspect, in ≤1 scene (≤2 procedural). [P1]
- [ ] Denouement ≤ 1 scene after the justice-or-escape beat. [P2]
- [ ] Subgenre content rules held (cozy: off-page violence, no profanity/sex; procedural: legal accuracy; domestic: no faked POV knowledge). [P1]
