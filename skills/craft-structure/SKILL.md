# Structure: beats, scenes, pacing, endings

Applies to: stage 3 (outline review of `book/outline/acts.md` + `book/outline/scenes.yaml`), stage 4 (drafting agents use the scene-craft section before writing any `book/manuscript/chNN/sNN.md`), stage 5 (scene-level checks in critiques), stage 7 (whole-book structural pass). Findings go to `book/critiques/chNN/<agent>-<date>.md` with quoted evidence and P0/P1/P2 severity. **NEVER** propose a change that contradicts a `## ⚓ Anchored` section of `book/bible/premise.md`; flag the tension instead.

## 0. Ask which model before diagnosing

Every mainstream framework converges on one skeleton: 25/50/25, early disruption, point-of-no-return at ~20–25 %, reversal at 50 %, lowest point at ~75 %, climax at ~85–90 %. Two deliberate alternatives exist and are not defects:

| Model | Shape | Reviewer stance |
|---|---|---|
| Kishōtenketsu (ki / shō / ten / ketsu) | Introduction, deepening without plot turns, a *twist that reframes* rather than escalates, balance restored | Tension comes from wondering how elements connect; payoff is understanding, not winning. Do not demand an antagonist or rising stakes. Do test that *ten* genuinely reframes *ki* and *shō*. |
| Jane Alison's shapes (wave, meander, spiral, radial/explosion, cell, network, fractal) | Pattern instead of swell-and-collapse arc; spirals common in reflective first person; radials circle a central wound or absence | Test for the declared pattern's integrity (does each return to the centre add something?), not for beat percentages. |

- **MUST** check `book/bible/premise.md` for a declared structural model. If none is declared and the outline does not fit the arc template, ask the author which model they are using *before* reporting "missing conflict", "no midpoint", or "no antagonist".
- Structure is a diagnostic, not a dictator. Percentages are targets; a beat 5 points off is not a finding. A beat *absent* or *not doing its job* is.

## 1. Consensus beat map (arc model)

The `book/outline/acts.md` beat table uses the positions below. For each beat: what it MUST accomplish, and the test a reviewer applies.

| % | Beat (names across frameworks) | MUST accomplish | Reviewer test |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–1 | Hook / Opening Image / Ordinary World | Named POV character with a discernible want, in a state that is the *opposite* of the resolution state; tone and voice signature | Can you state the opening state in one line and show it is inverted by the ending? |
| ~5 | Theme Stated | Counter-idea or controlling idea voiced once, usually by a secondary character, ideally one the protagonist disagrees with | Is the theme spoken more than once outright? If so, flag all but one. |
| 10–12 | Inciting Incident / Catalyst / Call | Disruption that introduces the main conflict and poses the story question | Is there a disruption by ~12 %? Name the dramatic question it raises. |
| ~20–25 | First Plot Point / Break Into Two / Crossing the Threshold / Doorway of No Return | Protagonist takes an irreversible action and commits; the Lie is first challenged; the dramatic question is clear by here | What does the protagonist do here that they cannot undo? If it could be undone, it is not the plot point. |
| ~22 | B Story | Relational/thematic thread begins; the character who will help the hero change enters | Is there a subplot carrying the thematic argument? |
| ~37 | First Pinch Point | Reader *sees or experiences* the antagonist's force on the page (a cutaway scene is acceptable); pressure, stakes shown | Point to the scene. "Hearing about" the antagonist fails the test. |
| ~50 | Midpoint / Second Plot Point | New information causes a paradigm shift; internal and external conflict converge; protagonist flips from reacting to acting; false victory or false defeat raises stakes; moment of clarity separating Lie from Truth | What does the protagonist *understand* now that they did not at 49 %? What do they *do differently* afterwards? Both answers required. |
| ~62 | Second Pinch Point | A whole scene showing the antagonist's nature, power and essence; protagonist realizes what is at stake; plan fails, villain seems to win | Point to the scene. Is the obstacle harder than at 37 %? |
| ~75 | Third Plot Point / All Is Lost / Ordeal | Genuine low: a cherished lie dies; the old plan/belief is finished; protagonist must decide whether to keep fighting | Name what dies. If the protagonist can simply try the same plan again, the low is not genuine. |
| 75–80 | Dark Night of the Soul | Reaction and reckoning (a sequel, not a scene) | Does it end in a decision, not just mood? |
| ~80 | Break Into Three / Plot Turn 2 | Hero realizes the key truth or receives the last piece of information needed to win | Is the realization earned by prior scenes (not a new character, not luck)? |
| 85–90 | Climax | Protagonist confronts the antagonist; the outcome of the goal is decided *by the protagonist acting on the Truth*; the story question posed at the hook is answered | Quote the sentence that answers the dramatic question. Does the protagonist's choice prove the arc? |
| 90–100 | Resolution / Final Image / Return with Elixir | Loose ends; irreversible character change shown in action; final image mirrors or contrasts the opening image | Is it short? Does at least one thread stay resonant rather than tidied? |

Irreversibility ladder (Story Grid): an irreversible action by the end of Act 1, a point of no return in the middle build, another irreversible action at the end of the middle build, an irreversible *character* change by the end. Four rungs; a missing rung is a P1.

Plan the Resolution first and work backward to the Hook (Seven-Point): the Hook state must be the opposite of the Resolution state or there is no arc.

Word budget for a 100,000-word novel: ~25k / 50k / 25k. Plan 50–60 scenes of ~2,000 words (minimum 15, five per section); the middle build gets half of them.

## 2. Scene craft (stages 4 and 5)

Two scene models, both enforced:

**Scene / Sequel (Swain, Bickham, Ingermanson)**
- Scene = Goal → Conflict → Setback. Sequel = Reaction → Dilemma → Decision.
- Sequels are legitimate and needed for pacing. **Flag any run of two or more consecutive sequels** with no goal-driven scene between them.
- Chapters are packaging; scenes are the unit. Do not judge structure by chapter breaks.

**Five Commandments (Story Grid)**, all five present in every scene, however subtly:
1. Inciting incident (causal: another person; or coincidental: an event)
2. Progressive complications, each harder than the last, never repeating a level, ending in a **turning point**: the last complication, after which the character must change tactics (new strategy) or change awareness (new understanding)
3. Crisis: the question the turning point poses, either *best bad choice* or *irreconcilable goods*
4. Climax: the action taken (action reveals character more than words)
5. Resolution: the outcome, which either closes the scene or becomes the next scene's inciting incident

**Value shift.** State it as "from X to Y" with polarity (safe → unsafe, trusted → betrayed, ignorant → knowing). If it cannot be stated, the passage is exposition with nothing happening, or a sequel masquerading as a scene. Core scenes shift the global value; others may shift a subplot value. A scene that begins and ends negative must end at a *different weight* of negative.

**Polarity alternation.** Values should alternate rather than stack (negative → double negative → triple negative reads as unrealistic). Across a chapter, check the sequence of `value_shift` polarities in `scenes.yaml`; three same-direction shifts in a row is a finding.

**Try/fail cycles.** Several failures, each harder than the last, before success. Outcomes are `yes-but` (goal achieved, with complication) or `no-and` (goal failed, and things get worse). Failures should arise naturally and reveal the character's limits, the antagonist's strength, or a knowledge gap. A run of clean successes is a finding; so is a run of failures at the same difficulty.

**In late, out early.** No arrivals, departures, greetings, small talk, weather. Leave as soon as the scene's goal is achieved or lost; unless it is the final scene, end with something unanswered.
- *Cut-the-opening test*: delete the first paragraph. Does the scene still work? Repeat until it does not; the last deletion that worked is the real entry point.
- Exception: a slower scene may give a breather or rebuild tension at the start of an act, but it must still turn.

Before:
> Mira parked, locked the car, and walked up the path. Inside, Teodor offered coffee. They talked about the weather while the kettle boiled. Then she asked about the will.

After:
> "You changed the will on the ninth," Mira said, before Teodor had finished pouring. "The day after the funeral."

**Egri's conflict taxonomy** for diagnosing why a scene feels dead: *static* (nobody can decide; drama stalls), *jumping* (the steps between emotional states are skipped; the shift feels unearned), *rising* (the result of a clear premise and uncompromising characters). Name which one.

### Does this scene earn its place? (maps to `scenes.yaml`)

Run for every scene at outline review (stage 3) against the manifest, and again at critique (stage 5) against the draft. Each answer must be one line; a blank is a finding.

| Step | Question | `scenes.yaml` field |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What does the POV character want in this scene? | `goal` |
| 2 | Who or what opposes it? | `conflict` |
| 3 | Where is the sentence at which the character must change tactics? (In the draft, quote it.) | `turn` |
| 4 | Did they get it? `yes-but` or `no-and`, and what is the "but"/"and"? | `outcome` |
| 5 | "From X to Y", with polarity | `value_shift` |
| 6 | Which beat or subplot does it serve? | `beat`, `subplot` |
| 7 | What does the next scene inherit from this one? (cause → effect) | next scene's `goal`/`conflict` |
| 8 | Could the first paragraph be cut? The last? | draft only |

- If 3 or 5 cannot be answered, the scene is exposition or a sequel; either convert it, merge it, or mark it as a sequel deliberately and check the two-sequel rule.
- If 7 cannot be answered, the scene is an island; the outline has a causal gap.
- Lisa Cron's matrix for the same check: external event → how the protagonist interprets it through the misbelief → what they do → the consequence the next scene inherits. The protagonist should be different at the end of every scene.
- Dialogue must do more than deliver information: a scene whose `turn` happens in dialogue needs the line that turns it.

## 3. Pacing and tension

Reader data: "slow and boring" was the top reason for abandoning a book in a Goodreads survey (46.4 % of respondents; the next two reasons were also slowness/boredom). Readers also complain when important moments are rushed. Both directions are pacing failures.

**Micro-tension (Maass).** Line-level unease the reader can only relieve by reading on. "Most writers don't have enough." Do not pause tension "for pacing"; vary its *kind* instead. Exercise: pick any paragraph and ask how to raise its tension (stronger verbs, nouns, metaphors with emotional charge). Environmental friction (a stuck latch, a crowd closing in) adds tension without stopping plot. Tension at a chapter's end comes from inside the POV character's emotional reaction, not from the event alone.

**Escalation vs repetition.** List every major obstacle in order. Each MUST be harder than the last; the same difficulty level never repeats. Stakes build gradually; a sudden jump feels unearned. List try/fail outcomes and verify a mix of yes-but and no-and.

**Sagging middle.** Causes: treating the middle as filler or delay, missing obstacles, no failed attempts, unplanned stretches (drafters without an outline run dry around 20–30k words), impatience to reach the climax. Fixes:
- Pinch points at 3/8 and 5/8 that put the antagonist on the page and raise stakes; "the simpler and more direct the pinch point the better".
- Midpoint as reflection: the protagonist reassesses what success means, then acts instead of reacts.
- Show what the character stands to lose.
- Give the middle its own try/fail cycle rather than a holding pattern.

**Surprise vs suspense (Hitchcock's bomb).** Surprise: nobody knows the bomb is there; the reader is startled for seconds. Suspense: the reader knows and the characters do not; anticipation drives every line until it goes off. Dramatic irony is suspense built from knowledge gaps. For each secret or reveal in `book/bible/clues.md` and `book/bible/timeline.md`, decide deliberately: does the reader learn it *before* the character (suspense) or *with* them (surprise)? Withhold, but hint in advance so the reader senses something missing; foreshadow every out-of-character choice.

**Chapter endings.** End on a question, a decision, or an emotional turn tied to the POV character's stakes. Reserve true cliffhangers; a withheld-outcome gimmick (cut away just before the answer) reads as a cheat. One cited case: a novella ending on an unresolved phone call made roughly 20 % of reviewers feel cheated and cost the author sequel sales. The *book's* ending **NEVER** withholds the answer to the central question.

**Scene length.** Commercial scenes run 1,000–5,000 words, ~2,000 typical (Ingermanson: 1–12 pages, average ~4). Vary length: a run of same-length scenes flattens rhythm; alternate intense and quiet. Check `word_target` across a chapter for monotony. Never let more than one consecutive scene pass with nothing at stake for the POV character.

## 4. Openings and endings

### First pages (stage 3 for scene 1 of the manifest; stage 5 and 7 for the draft)

Agents and editors decide within the first five to ten pages, sometimes within paragraphs. By page 1–2 the reader can answer *who is this about, what is it about, what is at stake*. Requirements:
- [ ] A named POV character doing something with a discernible want on page 1–2
- [ ] Begins at a point of change, not before it; "drop readers into an active scene"
- [ ] Something happens: action, problem, or interaction (not mostly interior thought)
- [ ] A sense of what could go wrong, and a tonal/voice signature, by page 5
- [ ] Catalyst by ~10 %
- [ ] Reader can follow what is happening; no more than ~3 named characters before the protagonist is anchored
- [ ] Opening image is a state the final image can mirror or invert

Red flags (any one is a P1 at stage 7):
waking up / alarm clock / ringing phone / getting out of bed; dream sequence; mirror self-description; weather small talk; opening with a flashback; backstory or worldbuilding dump before the main action; prologue infodump; too many characters at once; an ordinary crisis with no voice or angle; journal or stream-of-consciousness drift; confusing events with too little context; clichéd phrasing (hearts skipping, held breaths, chills down spines); overwritten, metaphor-heavy sentences. If the hook arrives late, cut what precedes it or hint conflict sooner.

### Endings (stage 3 against `acts.md`; stage 7 against the draft)

Surprising yet inevitable: only surprising is a cheat; only inevitable is predictable. The ending fulfils the hook's promises in an unexpected way, answers the central question, and restores order (or a new order). Ambiguous endings can satisfy; overly tidy ones ring false.

- [ ] Name the story question posed in Act 1 and quote the sentence where it is answered
- [ ] The climax requires the protagonist to act on the Truth learned, not luck, not a new character, not a rescue
- [ ] *"Because" chain*: from the Third Plot Point to the climax, every action follows the previous one via "because". Write the chain out. A link that needs "and then" instead of "because" is a gap.
- [ ] Emotional logic: feelings follow from events; the feeling at the climax was set up
- [ ] Meaning from change: the character ends different from the hook state; the change is shown in an action, not stated
- [ ] Final image mirrors or contrasts the opening image
- [ ] Every setup paid off, every payoff set up (promise → progress → payoff)
- [ ] Resolution is short and proportionate; no source gives a percentage, so test it by cutting: if the denouement still works a scene shorter, cut the scene
- [ ] At least one thematic thread remains resonant rather than resolved; "resonance, not resolution"

## 5. Theme as structure

`book/bible/premise.md` carries a controlling idea and a counter-idea. Both are structural tools, not decoration.

- Controlling idea: one cause-and-effect sentence, "[Value] prevails/fails when/because [cause]" (Egri's premise form: "Great love defies even death" = character / conflict / ending). One-word themes ("family") produce related-looking scenes with no shared meaning.
- Counter-idea: the opposite claim. The protagonist's Lie should equal the counter-idea and the Truth the controlling idea, so the arc *is* the argument. The antagonist embodies the counter-idea and must be able to argue it convincingly.
- Each scene's value shift proves either the idea or the counter-idea. The book alternates between them until the climax, where the idea wins (prescriptive story) or the counter-idea's victory is punished (cautionary story). **If the counter-idea never wins a scene, the book is one-sided and reads as preaching.** Tag each scene in `scenes.yaml` (`value_shift` polarity plus which side wins) and check the alternation.

Signs of preachiness (each is a finding):
- Characters discuss the theme at length while events do little
- The theme is stated outright more than once; keep at most one line, early (~5 %), ideally from someone the protagonist disagrees with
- Opposing views are weak or villainized; the antagonist has no believable case
- No inner struggle; goals met too easily
- Plot or character used as a soapbox

Fixes, in order: give the antagonist motivation and a voice; develop the protagonist's internal conflict first and let the theme emerge from it; show the theme in choices, events and recurring images, with dialogue alongside rather than instead. Stated themes are a primary "sounds AI-written" tell; treat them as a craft fault, not a style preference.

## 6. Revision order and when these checks run

Order: structural → character → scene → line → copy → proofread. Large changes delete polished prose, so **NEVER** report line-level findings on a scene that has an open structural finding; note "line pass deferred pending P0/P1 above" instead. A second draft cannot fix incoherence or dull storytelling; those are structural.

| Stage | This skill's job |
|---|---|
| 3 outline review | Beat map against `acts.md` (section 1); every scene through the earns-its-place table against `scenes.yaml` (section 2); polarity, try/fail and escalation sequence across the manifest (section 3); first-scene and ending checks against the plan (section 4); idea/counter-idea alternation (section 5). Confirm which structural model is declared (section 0). |
| 4 draft | Drafting agent reads section 2 before each scene; writes to the manifest's `goal`/`conflict`/`turn`/`outcome`/`value_shift`; applies in-late/out-early. |
| 5 critique | Scene-level checks on the drafted scene, quoting the turn sentence or its absence. |
| 7 book pass | Whole-book run of the checklist below over `acts.md`, `scenes.yaml`, `book/summaries/running.md` and the manuscript; first-pages red flags; "because" chain; opening/final image mirror. |

Findings quote evidence (the scene id and the lines), propose a diff (move, merge, cut, add a beat), and carry P0 (story does not work without it: missing commitment, midpoint, or climax that answers the question), P1 (structure works but sags or cheats: pinch point absent, repeated difficulty, stated theme, red-flag opening), P2 (polish: length monotony, a soft chapter ending).

## 7. Structural reviewer checklist

Run over `book/outline/acts.md` + `book/outline/scenes.yaml` (stage 3) or the manuscript (stage 7). Each item is answered with a scene id or a quote, or it is a finding.

- [ ] 1. Structural model declared in `premise.md` (arc / kishōtenketsu / Alison shape); checks below adjusted accordingly
- [ ] 2. Hook state stated in one line; resolution state is its opposite; final image mirrors or inverts opening image
- [ ] 3. Disruption by ~12 % that poses a single dramatic question, clear by the end of Act 1
- [ ] 4. Irreversible commitment by ~25 % (name the action that cannot be undone)
- [ ] 5. Antagonist's force *shown on the page* near 37 % and again, harder, near 62 %
- [ ] 6. Midpoint at ~50 %: what the protagonist now understands, and what they now do differently (both)
- [ ] 7. Genuine low at ~75 %: name the belief or plan that dies; the old plan cannot simply be retried
- [ ] 8. Climax at ~85–90 % decided by the protagonist acting on the Truth; quote the sentence that answers the dramatic question
- [ ] 9. "Because" chain from 75 % to climax has no "and then" links
- [ ] 10. Every scene has a statable goal, opposition, turn, yes-but/no-and outcome and "from X to Y" shift; islands (nothing inherited by the next scene) listed
- [ ] 11. No two consecutive sequels; no two consecutive scenes with nothing at stake for the POV character
- [ ] 12. Obstacles escalate, no repeated difficulty level; outcomes mix yes-but and no-and; polarities alternate
- [ ] 13. Controlling idea and counter-idea each win scenes; theme stated outright at most once
- [ ] 14. First pages: named POV character with a want by page 2, stakes and voice by page 5, none of the red-flag openings
- [ ] 15. Resolution short; the central question answered; at least one thread left resonant; every setup paid off
